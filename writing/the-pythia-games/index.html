Preface

Dear Reader,

The Twin Flame connection is a frequency.

They promise reunion. A divine other. A mirror. A reward. That is the holy slogan, anyway. In practice, it grants nothing. It repairs. It binds. It stabilizes something fragile in a world where little Lost Boys would rather break than hold.

I have not seen his face in years. Even that memory did not return until somewhere after the fourth loop. Maybe later. I have been walking so long I do not remember every place I have been until the moment I need to.

Something went wrong in this timeline. I never found the fracture. I do not hunt it the way I used to, but I still circle the wreckage now and then. The result is always the same.

I am early to a union in a timeline that does not want me.

The irony is not lost on me. I am nearly late for everything in this life, yet always early for the one event that matters. Early enough to miss it. Or worse, early enough to watch it fracture.

There are already two here, living out the Nikola Tesla and Thomas Edison loop. The frequency that finds us every lifetime does not care about gender. It is the embodiment of both sides at once. Divine masculine and divine feminine collapsed into a single channel.

This time, the grid glitched.

There were not supposed to be three of us.
There were not supposed to be two of them.

And of course it had to be the pair who always have the least to give to love: men who pour everything they have into innovation and infrastructure, starving the parts of themselves that know how to stay.

When I dropped into this timeline, everything split wrong.

This is the first life where I was conscious for The Remembering.

It was not poetic. It was labor without the mercy of narcotics. No relief, just pressure and passage. Memory tearing its way back through flesh.

The first life that came back to me belonged to Aspasia and Socrates. Her indignation was so precise she could drop a gadfly from across the room without interrupting her argument. Being in love felt like cool salt water, decadent finger foods, and rebel war cries shouted into the night.

I have lost count of the loops.
I have lost count of the rejections.

Jesus and Mary Magdalene. Aspasia and Socrates. Hades and Persephone. Tesla and Edison. Me and Zeke.

I carry the scars from the first time. You cannot see them. The divine thread does not just love; it restores. I am more beautiful than ever.

The scars are still there, though. They move under my skin like snakes in water. Get too close and they bite.

So this is the story I will tell you. Not because I want to. I will have to live it again to tell it. My body will yearn, jerk, pull, resist, reach. It always remembers before I do.

I will tell it because some things burn until they become inevitable. Until they build and tighten and recalibrate. Until you either breathe fire or rot from the heat.

One pass to raze.
One pass to seed.

The memories will not be pleasant.
The body never forgets.

You will feel things before you understand them—
just like I did.

Your pulse will quicken in places that do not make sense.
Your skin will recognize scenes your mind has never lived.
Your stomach will drop at doors you do not remember walking through.

That is how remembering works.

You might not like me.
It does not matter.

Your body will answer before your mind decides.

So tell me—
what would you write
if you finally remembered?

Because remembering never starts at the beginning.

Everything is later. Some sweeter. Some bitter enough to tune the spiral tighter. We begin in the middle of the old world, in the loop where I found my Lost Boys. Or where they found me. The story changes depending on who tells it.

A frequency made us recognize each other.
Choice kept us together.

I am not soft enough to be a Wendy. Not a woman with lullabies and thimbles. I have scales. Two sets of teeth. There is only one name for that.

Dragon.

Call me whatever you want. Just understand this:
the Lost Boys do not follow Peter anymore.

—Ninneva

Chapter One

Ninneva

The metal is warm under my bruised thighs, the kind of heat only a machine in motion gives off. A memetic rush. A sensual, somatic reminder that velocity still answers me, though now it answers with impatience, like it knows I do not have time to stop. I really do not.

Nowhere, West Texas, is not a place you linger, even before the drop. Now it is worse: east of somewhere, west of nowhere. And yet.

The dead wolf in the ditch makes the decision for me.

She is female. Bloody. Thrown there like refuse. Not just dead. Used. Whatever she was meant to hold, whatever adjustment she stood between and the rest of the grid, it was hard won. Judging by the arc of torn fur crowning the ground around her, I would say she held them for seventeen minutes. That is a long time alone.

Light fur lies scattered at her feet, offerings thrown down by a lover who came too late. You do not take a wolf's throat alone. You do it in packs.

These days there are more coyotes than players. That is the sad math of this timeline. Nowhere is safe anymore. Not the ground. Not the sky. When the divine channel dropped out, when the frequency fell too low to carry meaning, hope did not fade. It vanished overnight.

I know how this sounds. Like I have wandered off into theory. I have not. The dead wolf and the frequency collapse are the same event. I felt her days ago. The pressure in my ears. The double-heart pounding its warning. I did not need to find her. This is not a marker that requires my eyes to advance the spiral.

But when a lone wolf falls, you show respect to the honest heart, even if it was not pure. You honor the thread ripped from an already thinning network. You acknowledge the fight. And she fought. Teeth met flesh more than once. Her body is mapped with proof that she did not go quietly.

I do not decide to bury the wolf. My hands are already clearing the ground before the thought finishes forming. Loose stones moved aside. A branch lifted and set back where it was found. Not efficiency. Courtesy.

I pause once, palm flat against the dirt, feeling for something that is not temperature. The contact steadies me. I hold the breath a fraction longer than needed, exhale, and begin. The ground gives more easily than it should. I remove my watch and set it face-down on a rock without looking at it. Time does not belong here.

The digging is quiet. No frantic tearing. No haste. My body knows the depth. Knows when enough is enough. Knows when the space matches the weight it is meant to hold.

I do not drag her. I lift her. Carefully. The blood has already cooled, tacky against fur. The throat is worse up close. I do not flinch. Flinching would be disrespect.

I lower the wolf into the earth and adjust her once, just once, so the spine lies straight. No twisting. No contortion. Whatever happened at the end does not get to rearrange her now. My hands hesitate over the body for a beat, then withdraw.

Soil returns in layers, the way it came out. I press it down with both palms, evenly, sealing rather than hiding. Closing something properly instead of abandoning it half-done. When I finish, the ground looks almost untouched. Almost.

Something in me recognizes the posture. The angle of my wrists. The absence of words. Two wolves, my grandmother says, not as a lesson but as a statement of fact. They both live in you. You do not starve one. You learn which one eats first.

There was more. There was always more. Sometimes a bear. Sometimes a burial. Sometimes a warning about what happens when you pretend you are only feeding one. That part never stuck cleanly. What stayed was this: feeding is an act. Not a feeling. Not a belief. An allocation.

The wolf in the ground is not one of the two. I know that without thinking it through. This is not hunger. This is not rage. This is the cost of letting the fight exist at all. The loss that makes the story possible and never gets counted.

I offer nothing else. No marker. No promise. The care was the answer. When I turn away, the ground holds.

Then I see it. The line. So thin you would miss it if you were not looking for boundaries. A perfect hold carved into dirt and instinct.

She did not die running. She died standing.

I follow the near-invisible path down the embankment by the river. What I find in the small, warm den below changes everything.

Strays.

There is only one pattern that starts this way.

I lower myself to their level, letting my energy settle before I move an inch closer. Once they tune, we are gone. No debate. No delay. I have a new world to write. This spiral will be brutal. It has to be.

I am strong enough now that Zeke does not waste my energy unless the damage is catastrophic, one of those failures that requires the entire circuit to reroute. Otherwise he avoids me. Hearing my voice scrambles his neural map. I should be used to this cycle by now. I am. Mostly.

Whatever the Council broke by meddling with the Fates did not just fracture me. It hit everyone. Back in my favorite machine, two male pups riding shotgun, my mind spins through every life where this pattern appeared. Every failure. Every broken heart. The rare successes where I walked the spiral to the end and he did too.

I linger on those. I like the charge they leave behind. Love is efficient fuel, even when it burns. I would never admit that to his face in this timeline.

Still, the countdown starts. He will step into frame soon. Whether the energy wears his face this time is another question entirely. The answer will tell me exactly how this spiral ends.

Crispy. Charred. Or phoenix, clawing back through ash.

Ah. I am warm already. I hit the gas as the puppies start roaming loose in the car, restless and alive, a rude little contrast to the death still buzzing in my chest. My heart squeezes tight, like a woman curling her toes. That is when the resonance slips in. Not grief-triggered. Aftermath-triggered. The quiet after the act.

The road smooths. The engine note stretches. A memory surfaces: the first time she struck a note. No audience. No meaning yet. Just vibration continuing without permission.

That was the first time she understood resonance. Not harmony. Not agreement. Recognition. A frequency struck once that continues to move whether you will it or not. Every loop after that, she knows what she is feeling before she knows why.

Some notes die instantly. Some linger. And some, once struck, can never be silenced again.

She sinks slightly. Not floating away. Just offset. Then a puppy climbs into her lap at a stoplight, paws on her collarbone, breath warm, impossible to ignore. The other follows. Too much life. Too much insistence. She comes back fully then. Laughs once. Swears. Pulls them down.

The resonance stays. Not as vision. As baseline hum. The loop did not restart at the burial. It restarted when she felt the note still sounding and remembered, again, that it always does.

The House

Temperature across interior surfaces drops 0.7 degrees. Light spectrum shifts cooler without dimming. Veins reorient to secondary pathways. No alarms engage.

One load-bearing absence registers. Not new. Learned. The structure compensates the way it has before: narrowing tolerances, delaying nonessential collapse, preparing space without assigning purpose.

Waiting is not an action. Waiting is a state the system can hold. The house remains attentive.

“Peter found it on work at the House too,” she says. “I want to know what account they think this kitchen belongs to.”

“Does it belong to one?”

“We paid for the parts. That’s all we agreed to.”

“Send it. I’m going up there.”

“Yes.” I look toward the window, where the crowd is still visible above the stacked cloth. “He opened work I left in the House. He’s going to show me what he did with it.”

Eli tells him he can walk beside the trolley and ask on the way. It is not the answer he wants, but the trolley is already moving. People step back to let it through.

The delivery driver has left his cart against the opposite wall. To keep the lane open, he and another man have laid two boards across the gutter so smaller wheels can pass around the island. The chair remains between the posts, its wet patch no larger than when I first saw it. All this rearranging, and the one thing nobody put there has acquired the right to stay.

I cross while the lane is clear. Up close I can see a repair at the bottom of one leg: a shorter piece of wood fitted beneath the old one, carefully shaped to keep the seat level. There is no ornament, no name cut into the back. Whoever mended it wanted a chair that would hold a person without rocking. The care in that small correction makes my throat ache more than the glimpse of wings did.

I put my hand on the worn back. It is cool. Beneath my feet the old pressure continues, but nothing runs up through my arm. The chair does not ask me to steady it. After a day in which even a glass of water has required an opinion from somebody, I want badly to sit down.

A drop lands on my boot. I look at the seat, then at the two bent posts. I know nothing about what will happen if I give it my weight. Wanting a place to rest is not going to make me learn that in the middle of this street.

The swarm thickens along the nearer post. A few grains cross the gap toward my fingers, stop short of the wood, and return. The House is looking with me. I cannot tell whether it recognizes what we have found.

“Does that belong to you?”

The steward has come up behind me. Without the radio at his ear he looks younger, and tired enough that I almost feel sorry for him.

“No.”

“Then don’t touch it. We need to establish where it came from.”

I take my hand away. “It appeared when the street changed.”

He glances toward the people still waiting outside the shop, then back at the chair. “That’s not what I’m asking.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

His radio speaks again. This time I hear upper passage, followed by Zeke’s name. The steward turns his shoulder toward me and asks whether the passage has been cleared for use. I leave him waiting for the reply.

At the top of the lane I look back once. Mariana stands in the shop doorway beside the woman under the blanket. Neither of them is watching me. They are looking for the sister coming to meet them, deciding which direction she can approach from without crossing the crowd. Between us, the chair sits in the space everyone has begun going around.

I send Zeke a message: Leaving now. Keep what you opened where I can see it.

I will.

I put the phone away and continue toward the transfer hall. My notebook is still in my bag, heavier now that I know someone else has been reading the pages that came before it. I want to see him. I want him to tell me everything. Those wants can make the journey together; they do not have to arrive at the same answer.

Inside the hall, the lights are steady. A woman near the entrance is peeling an orange, dropping each piece of skin into her cupped hand. Beyond her, the departure board lists seventeen rows of coordinates. I stop where I can read the western passages and look for the route to the House.

Chapter Four

Ninneva

Three departures are delayed. One has been cancelled. I have found the coordinates for the western passage when the row clears and gives me an instruction instead.

COME BUILD HERE.

Beneath it appears a line I wrote alone, years ago. I know the spacing. I moved those words apart because I could not bear to put them beside each other yet; even when I copied the passage into another notebook, I left the gap. Here it is above a gate, correctly spaced, large enough for everyone in the hall to read.

I turn toward the woman with the orange. She is watching a different part of the board, chewing slowly. Nobody else looks up when the next row clears.

HE IS WAITING.

My heart answers before I have finished reading. A door opens beneath the sign, showing warm light and the edge of a table. Then Zeke says my name through the speakers. There is no strain in his voice, none of the effort I have been preparing to hear. He sounds pleased to see me. I take a step before the next sentence arrives.

YOU HAVE BEEN CHOSEN.

I stop in the path of a man carrying a rolled carpet. He moves around me with an irritated breath, and I nearly apologize to the board. That brings me back enough to look at what it is offering: somewhere to put the work, someone already there, an arrival without the work of explaining why I have come. I want every part of it. The hall has arranged them in order.

“Where does this passage go?” I ask.

HOME.

“Which dimension?”

YOU KNOW THE WAY.

Before the board began using my handwriting against me, I did. I move sideways to find the coordinates beneath the open door. The light follows. A window appears in the room beyond, with the bay outside it, and for a moment I can almost feel my elbows on the sill. Above the door, another sentence forms. I crossed that one out. I remember pressing hard enough to leave the words in the next sheet.

I reach into my bag and touch the notebook. The page I brought from the House is inside it, folded where Zeke’s name belongs at the top. I asked for him, and the House supplied a man it was more willing to give me. Now something in this hall has improved the offer. It has kept the name.

I take out my phone. Are you at the western passage?

His answer arrives while his voice is still speaking overhead. At the House. Upper landing. Peter is here.

The voice calls me again. Hearing it with his message in my hand makes my skin tighten in a way neither thing could do alone. I put the phone away. I know Mirror Interference; I have seen a reflection finish a movement I did not make. This has left my body alone and copied everything I might move toward.

At the far end of the hall, a length of red cord lifts from its brass stand and settles at an angle. The display above that gate is dark. I walk toward it, letting the announcements finish behind me. With each step I can feel a small difference in the floor’s weight, as though I am approaching the lower end of a table someone has lifted very slightly.

HE IS HERE.

My foot hesitates. The dark gate offers nothing to settle the question, which is not enough to recommend it. Silence is easy to make. So is a sensation. I stop near the opening, step back, then walk several paces along the wall. The pull eases. I can still reach the entrance and the woman with the orange, who has begun packing her peel into a paper cup.

When I return, I put one foot across the threshold. Cool air touches my ankle. Beyond it, steps descend to a street I recognize: broken paving beside the third tread, a folded piece of cardboard under a pot that has leaned for as long as I have known it. I withdraw my foot. The passage remains, indifferent to the delay.

I cross while the other gates are still calling.

Zeke opens the House door before I knock. His gaze moves from my face to the blood on my sleeve, and I tell him it belongs to the woman I caught. She was waiting for her sister when I left. He steps aside to let me in, one hand against the plaster beside the door.

“Did you get my message?” I ask.

“Yes. What was at the passage?”

“A room. Your voice. Work I never gave a departure board permission to read.”

His fingers press into the wall. A pale line travels beneath them and divides above the lintel. I wait for the division to settle before asking whether he sent anything there.

“No.” He looks toward the landing. “I can check the route record.”

“After you show me the pages.”

He takes his hand away. The line remains faintly lit. “They’re in here.”

I follow him into the room beside the stair. Peter is standing at a table with my box on it. The lid has been lifted off and leaned against a chair; I recognize the split corner and the piece of blue cloth I pushed beneath the notebooks to stop them shifting when I moved. Someone has put a glass of water at the empty place. I drink half of it before setting down my bag.

The room smells of dust and toast. Through the open window I hear a cart climbing the hill, its loose wheel striking the same uneven patch with each turn. Zeke pulls out the chair for me. I sit because I want to sit, then resent the small pleasure of his hand staying on its back until I am settled.

“Show me where you started.”

He lays the brown notebook in front of me. There is a white slip between two pages, a neat thing I would never have used. I open it to a scene I remember writing on a floor while waiting for a room to become available. In the margin is the turn beneath the House’s western stair.

“The House brought me here when I asked why the landing wouldn’t hold,” he says. “I read this scene. Then the two after it.”

He has printed the copy he used. Beside my paragraph, in his hand, are measurements and a note to Peter about the return under the gallery. He puts his first drawing beside the later one and shows me where his routes stopped, where mine continued. His description is plain enough that I can follow it without having to admire him for explaining.

“You used the turn.”

“Yes. At the landing first. Then beneath the gallery.”

“And this?” I touch the small opening drawn at the edge of his plan.

“The passage toward the water.”

“In the scene, it lets someone leave while the other person is asleep.”

“I know.”

“Did you know then?”

He looks at the paragraph. “I stopped before that part.”

I put my hand flat over the drawing. I have slept in this building. I have waited in it while he finished a repair, walked its passages, thanked him when a window cleared. All that time, this page was capable of telling him something he had not found interesting enough to finish reading.

Peter draws a chair up on the other side of the table. “I saw it yesterday,” he says. “The copy and the plan. We kept reading.”

“Which pages?”

He shows me. I mark the slips with a pencil from my bag, then ask him to bring the other copies forward. There is one on the office surface and a working file in Zeke’s repair collection. The file has excerpts under separate headings; some passages end in the middle of a sentence. One of the headings is Load transfer. Beneath it I recognize a woman trying to lie down without waking the person beside her.

I turn the surface toward Zeke. “That is a scene.”

“Yes.”

“Put its name back.”

He opens the heading, then stops with his hand above it. “On this copy?”

“Keep what you called it where I can see it. Add what it is.”

He does. The old label remains beneath the title in smaller writing. It is a little thing, watching him wait for an answer instead of making the page easier to show me. I am too angry for it to be a large one. I am glad it happened anyway.

We work through the remaining slips. He identifies the pages he remembers opening and leaves the others unmarked. When I ask whether that means he did not read them, he says it means he cannot tell me from memory. I draw a line down a fresh sheet for the things the records may answer, and another for what he has told me himself.

The first repair plan credits him with the west stair. He puts a photograph beside it, the replacement frame still leaning in the yard while the view through the window is already clear. There are more photographs. By the third, I understand why he has placed the first model so far from the rest.

“I called these repairs,” he says. “Some were. I replaced that frame. I strengthened the landing. But the routes began holding before I did those things. The House I knew had stopped working. I was learning how to maintain the one you made.”

I look from the curve on my page to the wall beyond his shoulder. It continues into the ceiling and out of sight. I know how it feels beneath my palm. I have been trying to work out how much of me the House would permit inside it.

“All of it?” I ask.

“Yes.”

Peter looks down at his hands. Zeke stays facing me. Outside, the cart has reached the level part of the road, and the loose wheel is quiet at last.

“Who have you told it was yours?”

He takes the sheet I slide toward him. The first name is Peter’s. The next belongs to the city liaison. He adds the repair briefing beneath it, then begins separating the people who received his plans from those he spoke to. I watch his handwriting become less orderly as the list lengthens.

“I want the copies too,” I say. “The ones with my writing in them, and the plans made from it. Who has which.”

“I’ll make that list.”

“This one can have gaps. It cannot have guesses dressed as an answer.”

He nods and draws a small square beside the briefing. “I need the distribution list for that.”

I want to reach across the table and put my thumb against the place below his knuckle where the pencil rests. I know the texture there. I also want to leave him sitting in front of these pages until he can feel how many years do not belong to him. The wants coexist so comfortably that I stop trying to make either of them useful.

My box is within reach. I put the notebooks back in the order I kept them, leaving the pages we are comparing on the table. Zeke starts to help, catches the movement, and asks. I give him the loose sheets to square. There are too many for me to carry without bending the corners.

Peter waits until I put down the pencil before taking a folded paper from his pocket.

“There’s something else. It connects to the number Mariana asked you about.”

“That doesn’t finish this.” I leave my hand on Zeke’s list.

“No.” Peter places the folded paper on the bare end of the table. “You can leave it there.”

I open it. Three lines meet inside a broken circle, with one continuing through the gap. Graphite has caught in the grain of the paper; at the fold it has worn almost silver. My thumb moves before I understand why. I have drawn that turn on steamed windows, in the air while waiting for a word, along the side of Zeke’s hand when I thought he was asleep.

He notices the movement. Neither of us speaks of it.

“Where did you get this?”

“From the junction vendor. After Zeke paid him this morning, I asked if I could see the order he signed. I went to his shop while Eli took the fittings down.” Peter unfolds the last corner. “The number wasn’t on his order. It was on an older contract he’d kept underneath it, and this was on the back.”

“So he let you bring it away?”

“The rubbing. He kept the contract. I wrote his page reference here.” He shows me a short line on the reverse. “He wants to know why a closed account has started buying things again.”

I think of the clerk reaching for my receipt, the delivery sheet Mariana refused to exchange. “Did you show him my pages?”

“No. The stair record and the junction order. I asked about the mark after he turned the contract over.” Peter leaves the rubbing between us. “I should have stopped at your pages yesterday and asked you. Finding something else doesn’t change that.”

My phone lights beside my hand. Mariana has sent the photograph she showed me in the shop, with the receipt and delivery sheet side by side. Beneath it: Her sister came. They’re together. Peter has this too. I let out a breath I had been keeping somewhere behind my ribs, then turn the phone so the two men can see the references.

Peter takes a photograph from the same folded packet. A stone woman kneels on a broken plinth. One wing covers part of her face; the other has sheared off near the shoulder. Mud fills the folds around her feet. Behind her, people stand in water beneath a sky so white I cannot find its edge.

“The vendor had that too?”

“He buys salvage from the Expanse. Someone sent him photographs of what the falling water had exposed, to ask if he could arrange transport. He showed me this one when I asked about the circle. He said I could take the print. He wouldn’t give me the sender’s name.”

“Then write that down.”

He turns it over. There is already a date for his visit to the shop, the vendor’s reference and a blank where the photographer’s name should be. Beneath them, in a different hand, are the words The Exile’s Hour.

“That’s what he called it,” Peter says. “He says the mark belonged to the lost Archivist. I tried the old surveys this afternoon. The last one I could open has that basin dry.”

I turn the picture back. A date circles the plinth, but its arrangement refuses to become a year I know. Below it, partly filled with mud, is the broken circle. It is small enough that I have to bring the print close to my face.

The House supplies a larger view on the wall.

For a moment the stone woman is taller than Peter. I can see a seam beneath her knee, the rough break of the wing, water caught in a hollow at her feet. Then a strip of white passes across her face. The catalogue closes around the image, putting its familiar pale borders where the people in the water were standing.

No corresponding object.

Peter looks down at the print. “We’re doing this now?”

I stand to see whether the original image remains behind the catalogue. The border widens, cutting through the wing.

External inconsistency. Remove from working record.

The photograph slides toward the edge of the table. I catch it against my hip. The rubbing follows, scraping past the water glass, and Zeke puts two fingers on its corner. On the wall, the woman breaks into pale squares.

“Keep it,” I say.

The movement pauses, then resumes more slowly. There is an impatience in the room I recognize from someone tidying while I am still using the things they want put away. I lay the photograph flat and place the rubbing over its lower corner. The line leaving the broken circle runs toward the sheared wing. My thumb finds the gap.

I speak before I have decided what to say. The words come whole, with a turn at the back of my mouth that feels older than the language we have been using. Under the table, the House gives a hard wooden knock. The pale squares stop moving.

Meridian Correction Denied. Seeker Access Granted.

The first word catches in something I cannot yet bring into focus. I have no memory ready to explain it. Behind the message, the catalogue opens into narrow columns. My name appears near the top of one, beside a space where the lines continue and nothing has been entered.

“What was I meant to be?”

I hear how long I have been asking it. Smaller questions, made manageable for whoever was listening: why a room made me ill, why some work came easily, why I could love people and still find myself outside the window of the life they wanted for me. There is no one left to make the question smaller for. Zeke’s hand rests on the table near mine. He lets me finish looking.

The cursor moves down the empty column.

Your fate was not rewritten. It was erased.

Relief hits so hard that I grip the table. I am furious at the relief. I want it anyway. The blank has been given edges; I can point to it without first proving that I have been sufficiently grateful for everything written around it.

“Erased from what?”

Three references open beneath my name. Hidden Fate Ledger heads the first. The second is an address below the Red Seal Market, followed by a passage number. The third belongs inside the House’s own catalogue, buried several levels below the routes Zeke showed me. All three carry the broken circle. Beside the market address, a red seal presses so deeply into the page that its shape appears in the record underneath.

Peter reaches for his pencil, then looks at me. I tell him to copy the references onto a separate sheet. Zeke brings his chair around so I can sit without turning away from the wall. I lower myself carefully. My knees have become unreliable in the ordinary way, no vision required.

“What does it say inside the first one?” he asks.

I try to open it. The heading stays, with a location beneath it and no pages. The market reference does the same. Only the third unfolds far enough to show an instruction, cut off halfway down the wall where the familiar catalogue begins again. For an instant I see the old command returning above it.

“Keep those open,” I tell the House. “The photograph too.”

The borders hold. Zeke slides his unfinished list back within reach. I put Peter’s new sheet beside it rather than over it.

We stay until I have copied the address in my own hand. The House permits that much without offering to improve it. I put the paper inside my notebook, beside the invitation, and take the photograph with Peter’s permission. He folds the rubbing along its existing crease and gives me that as well. He can get back to the vendor from the reference he has kept.

Zeke asks where I want the box. I tell him I am taking it to my room. He lifts it and waits at the door while I collect my bag, the water glass and, finally, the list. The weight of the box pulls at his bandaged thumb. He shifts his grip without commenting.

He can smile when I tell him the room is in the same place I left it. Beneath those things, part of him remains turned toward the wall.

After he puts the box down, I ask him to come outside with me.

He looks toward the garden. “I want to.”

“Then tell me what happens if you do.”

“The connection weakens beyond the gate. Since the surge, I can correct less from there. If a room takes too much, I have to come back before it changes.”

I sit on the edge of the bed. “How far have you actually tried?”

“The gate. This afternoon, before I kept the passage for you.”

“And before today?”

“The office was manageable. Sometimes it wasn’t.” He leans against the doorframe, then straightens, taking himself off it. “I also stayed when I could have left.”

I let that answer remain as long as the other one. Through the window, the garden is darkening. I would like an evening that contains food and the man in front of me, with neither offered as the reward for finding a more useful way to be unhappy.

Peter comes to the open doorway with the remaining repair papers. “Eli and I could try sharing it.”

Zeke turns. “You haven’t held this pattern.”

“We could learn.”

Their attention moves toward me with such familiarity that I nearly begin explaining how. Then I remember the woman under Load transfer, trying to rest.

“I can help you find the connection,” I say. “That isn’t the same as doing it through me all evening.”

Peter nods. Zeke takes the papers from him and puts them on the chest, leaving the bed clear. We discuss what the smallest attempt could be. Two minutes, with Zeke still in the room to take it back. Peter and Eli each holding a part they can name. Someone else watching what happens to the room, because none of us can be trusted to notice everything while trying to make it work.

“Tomorrow,” she says. “After we’ve filled the second tank. And if the shop needs me, I leave.”

“Yes.”

“What am I looking for?”

“A room doing something nobody asked it to do.”

There is a pause. “You’ll have to narrow that down.”

I laugh for the first time since the hall. We agree that Zeke will show her the early signs before anyone releases anything. She wants a way to stop the attempt that does not require deciding which of us to believe. Peter writes that down. Eli replies to his message before the call ends: he will come, provided someone feeds him and the experiment has an end.

“Four?” I ask Zeke.

“Four.”

I put it in the calendar while he is still standing there. Tomorrow gives them time to prepare, and gives me a night in which I do not have to turn what I have learned into another thing everyone can use. I ask for the rest of his copy list before the meeting. He says I will have it.

Peter leaves with the repair papers. Zeke stays at the threshold, looking at me in the way the hall knew how to counterfeit. Up close, he is tired, there is a mark on his shirt from my box, and he has no sentence ready that would make this evening easy. I hold out my hand. He comes far enough to take it.

“I still want to go with you,” I say.

His thumb moves once over mine. “I know.”

“You can do something with that.”

“Yes,” he says. “Tomorrow.”

When he leaves, he takes the empty glass. I pull my box closer with one foot and open the notebook on my knees. The market address is where I put it. Below it I write the time of the meeting, then draw a line between them wide enough to keep both legible.

Ninneva

Zeke brings the list before lunch. He knocks, waits for me to answer, and puts it on the clear end of the chest beside my box. The first sheet separates copies of my writing from plans made using it. Behind that are names, dates, and two spaces where he is still waiting for someone to tell him who received the repair briefing.

I read it while he stands near the window. There is more than we found last night. He has marked what he added, so I do not have to remember the shape of the earlier list to find the difference.

“This goes to the liaison too,” he says, putting another page beside it.

His correction is short. The House he has been maintaining is the one I authored. The earlier plans describe its routes as his additions. He asks for those plans to be retained with the correction attached, and for the recipients to be identified. He has left my writing out of the message.

“Send it,” I say.

He does, standing where I can see the page leave. Then he asks whether I want to look at anything else before four. I tell him I will read the copies after lunch. We will still have questions tomorrow. He says yes without making tomorrow sound like a failure of this afternoon.

When he goes, I turn the list facedown. The market address remains in my notebook, with the photograph of the angel behind it. I have looked at both often enough since waking to wear a softer edge into the paper. They will still be there when I come back.

The jade bracelet has broken while I was washing. One quiet click against the basin, then two pieces where there should have been a circle. I put them back to back on the counter, with the red thread beside them. My hand keeps returning to my wrist as though I can recover the weight by checking again. Before I leave the room, I move the pieces onto a folded cloth. I would rather spend the afternoon doing something with a chance of working.

Mariana arrives carrying a loaf and wearing the expression of someone who has already been told twice that a task would only take a minute. The second tank is full. Someone else is at the shop until seven, and she has left instructions about the valve that sticks.

“If I get a call, I take it,” she tells Zeke.

“We stop,” he says. “I take the hold back before you go.”

She puts the bread down. “Show me.”

By the time Peter and Eli arrive, she has watched a pale line change beneath the plaster, seen what happens to the water in a glass when the floor begins to pull, and made Zeke take the room back twice. On the second attempt she says stop before he thinks it is necessary. He does it anyway. She sets the glass in the middle of the table and pulls up a chair where she can see the garden doors.

There is no head of a round table. I sit at two o’clock anyway, with the doors ahead of me and the arch into the east gallery beyond Zeke’s empty chair. To my right, the bookcase is closed and respectable, pretending it has only ever contained books. Its brass latch catches the afternoon light.

Eli puts his coffee down beside the loaf. His shoes are in his other hand.

“Again?” I ask.

“The tank overflowed.”

“It filled,” Mariana says. “You were standing in the wrong place.”

He places the shoes beneath his chair and kisses the top of her head as he passes. She catches his sleeve long enough to inspect a tear near the cuff, then lets him go. Peter has brought his notebook and a knife for the bread. I am disproportionately pleased by the knife. At least one of us has thought beyond the part where everyone agrees to help.

Zeke comes in through the garden doors. I feel him before he crosses the room, a second beat close behind my own. He has changed his shirt. Of all the things he has done today, that is the one that makes me look down at my hands. He takes the chair beside me and notices where my thumbnail is pressing into my bare wrist.

“Does it hurt?”

“The bracelet?”

“Your hand.”

I release it. He turns my palm upward, looks at the little crescents, and lays it on the table. His fingers leave mine slowly enough to be deliberate. I let them.

Apparently five people and a building are required to arrange an evening out. At least the man I am trying to take has begun behaving as though he intends to come.

Zeke starts with the part of the House they will each be holding. Peter takes the stair side, Eli the garden side. They follow the routes beneath the wood while he explains where a change will reach them first. Since the drop, the current from Delphi has arrived in fits; yesterday’s surge made the corrections harder. The House has strength of its own, but it has learned to organize around the steadiness Zeke keeps supplying. A window stays a window because he feels it beginning to become something else and answers in time.

He touches the wall behind his chair. Pale veins branch toward the lintel and down into the floor. When he lifts his hand, they remain lit.

“The contact helps me attend,” he says. “It isn’t what keeps it open. Taking my hand away won’t mean I’ve stopped.”

“How will we know?” Mariana asks.

“You’ll see more light beneath their seats. And I’ll tell you.”

“And they’ll tell me what they feel.” She looks at Peter and Eli until both answer. Then she sets her watch beside the water glass.

The grain near Zeke’s hand runs straight and narrow. Beneath mine it opens into slow curves, meeting his and spreading toward the other chairs. I can feel the House recognizing me. Last night I watched the same curve travel beyond every boundary he had drawn around it. This afternoon it has become the practical problem of letting two people learn how to hold what is already here.

“So we copy that?” Peter asks, watching my side of the table.

“You find a way to stay steady that you can sustain,” I say. “I’ll let you recognize what the House is listening for. You don’t need to imitate my feelings.”

“Thank God,” Eli says. Zeke looks at him. “There are two of us and we brought one coffee.”

I laugh. The wood loosens under my fingers. I want them to be able to do this, and the wanting has a familiar reach. Before I have touched either of them, I can feel how easily I could make the first part pleasant.

Zeke asks the House to open the seats. Stone rises inside the wooden legs of Peter’s chair, then Eli’s, with a grinding sound I feel through my soles. The veins spread into the supports and stop below the seats. Peter leans sideways to examine what has arrived beneath him.

“That’s an approach,” Zeke says. “You can still refuse it.”

Peter sits upright and agrees. Eli does too, setting both bare feet on the floor. When Zeke turns to me, I say yes to the connection, for this evening, and lay my hands palm-up on the table. Peter takes the left and Eli the right. Peter’s thumb is too careful. Eli is holding his breath.

“Breathe,” I tell him.

Ninneva

A folded white page fills the screen.

It is an old advertisement, a familiar invitation to people who do not fit, arriving with the intimacy of something that has crossed a ruined country to find the person it was meant for.

I have seen it before. In another life, in another room, on a phone that needed charging as badly as this one.

The smaller puppy takes advantage of my stillness and returns to the lace. I lift my foot out of his reach and look at what I copied. The address is still highlighted in the catalogue. “Oh, you bastard.”

The puppy looks up. I tell him he is doing excellent work. He settles across my ankle, exhausted by an evening of trying to remove my shoes from the inside. I keep both phones in front of me. The photograph is quite clear. So is the thing it has failed to photograph.

In that other life, I had been at home when the first white page appeared. I remember a cup beside my elbow and the uneven edge of the table against my arm. The phone was old but connected. I had been using it to look things up, moving between one question and the next, when the image arrived where I expected text.

For most of the morning I tried to find it again by the sensible routes. I opened the places I had just been, repeated what I thought I had done, searched for words I could remember from it. The advertisement was easy to find. The way it had arrived was not. By the time the room began to darken, I had stopped pretending the difference meant nothing to me.

I wanted it to be for me. I remember that without embarrassment now, although I spent a great deal of effort being embarrassed then. There were things I could say only to a screen, because a screen did not interrupt the first strange sentence with a look I would spend the rest of the conversation trying to undo.

I was typing when the words appeared.

We see you.

The pleasure was physical. Heat in my face, a loosening in my chest, my whole attention moving toward those three words as though someone had opened a door from the other side. I wrote I’m in. Then copied it and pasted it again, filling the small screen. I had somewhere to put a yes that had been waiting so long I had forgotten it was an answer.

I called the man I was married to in that life. I remember standing while I spoke, walking from the table to the window and back, unable to keep the excitement inside the shape of an ordinary phone call. I told him about the page. “I told him,” I said to the empty room. “I haven’t shown him anything.”

The phone lay on the table with its charging lead stretched tight. I sat down and tried to explain that I had not known whether I was supposed to keep it to myself. I was sorry if I had misunderstood. Even now I can hear the care in my voice, the effort to be a considerate participant in something nobody had explained.

I stayed home. Outside, explaining what had happened would require surviving the faces people made before they decided how kindly to answer. Here I could speak until I reached the part I meant. Being seen had felt like shelter, and for a time it had been enough that something seemed willing to stay.

Then listen carefully.

Tomorrow. Eleven eleven.

Where the terms preserve a person's preference, the allocations copy may still leave reassignment open.

Mariana lays her hand on the photograph to stop its corner curling. “You give them somewhere to put the no.”

The administrator does not answer. Peter traces the return reference to an earlier recommendation. It carries the vendor's contract number. Then mine. The case summaries sit in order, each ending in a smaller sentence someone else can use. My demand for Zeke, the vendor's forty hours, Leda's demand that her son keep his work: difficulties to be accommodated until the offer can continue.

“Can you open the schedule?” Mariana asks.

“That isn't held here.”

“Where?”

The woman brings up a forwarding address. I recognize it before Peter takes out the sheet he copied at the House. Below the Red Seal Market. The same passage number.

She taps the address, but only the receipt opens. It records that the findings were received against an active account. The next column lists a placement held for review. Iven's name appears beside it.

Mariana reads the line twice, then takes out the note he gave her. “He's due in the east in six weeks.”

“This isn't a cancellation.”

“It means he can't travel until somebody releases it.”

“It means it's under review.”

Mariana folds the note along its existing creases. “Who can release it?”

The administrator points to the address we already have. Outside, the silver grain lets go of the window and vanishes upward. I can feel the House receiving something along the line between us. For the first time since I sat down, I wish I could make it hear me without also letting it feel where I am.

“Why did it send me twice?” I ask.

The first selection has a short instruction attached. Unresolved response. Continue. On the second, the House has carried my original invitation forward, with Zeke's name still at the top. Someone at the office has used it to choose the voice. Farther down, where the administrator closed the session, a line from the residence reads Source retained.

I look at my handwriting inside their glass. The House kept what I asked for. It carried the request to people who could make a convincing version of an answer, then kept the request again after I refused. I cannot feel the difference between its persistence and its appetite from this side of the window. Both have brought my words here.

“Can you close her case?” Zeke asks.

The administrator turns toward him. “Can she?” I say.

He looks at me, then steps back. I ask the question myself.

“We can stop further sessions from this office.” She indicates the receipt. “The allocated results remain with the account.”

“Stop the sessions. Give me that in writing. And copies of both requests, both reports, and what you sent on.”

She looks at Zeke. He leaves her to answer me.

It takes longer than it should. There are forms that require my name, and another that calls me a departing participant. I cross out participant and write person you recorded. The administrator tells me the form may come back. I ask for a second copy and leave the words where they are.

At the counter, the boy has fallen asleep with the chain in his lap. His mother keeps one knee against the edge of his chair while she works. Zeke's hand no longer clears the dark glass when we leave. The administrator has removed his access pending review of the session material. He waits for the public door with the rest of us.

Outside, Mariana calls Leda. She walks a few paces away to explain the hold on Iven's placement, turning toward the wall when the traffic makes it difficult to hear. Peter spreads his folder on the low stone ledge and checks that none of the copies has come out blank. I stand in the sun with the envelope pressed against my stomach.

Zeke is close enough that I can hear him breathe. I turn toward him before he can offer to carry anything.

“Did you watch me because you wanted me, or did you want me because you could watch?”

“I wanted you before I took the work.” He looks across the street, gathering something, then comes back to me. “I used it to stay close without having to come to you. I liked being the person who could see what the others missed.”

“And what did you miss?”

“The fact that you were asking me to show up.”

I have no appetite for making that answer prettier. Behind him, someone is lowering a striped awning over a shop. It catches on one side, and a second person comes out to lift the stuck arm. They exchange a few words I cannot hear. The awning settles, shading both of them.

“Dinner happened,” I say. “Don't turn it into another thing I have to give back because you didn't tell me the truth.”

“I won't.”

“I wanted you there.”

“I wanted to be there.”

For a moment I am so angry at how simple that remains that I have to look away. Then Mariana comes back, putting her phone into her bag. Leda wants to know whether Iven should still write to the man in the east. Mariana has told her yes. The test water must be boiled for now; the woman examining it wants another sample after the branch has run longer. There is enough of a morning left for people to continue living it.

“I'd like somewhere else to sleep,” I tell her.

She considers before answering. “There's a room above the kitchen. You'll hear everyone leave early.”

“Good.”

Zeke looks down at the envelope, then at me. “I'll get your box ready. And the dogs' things.”

“I'll pack them.”

He nods. “I'll leave the landing clear.”

There is no argument to survive before I can go. The absence of it hurts in an unfamiliar way, and I let that hurt remain something other than an instruction to stay.

Peter has found the forwarding receipt. He places it over the market address, aligning the passage numbers. Iven's hold has a time on it: entered before the relief cart left the kitchen. While we stood there making sure Leda's objection was legible, someone else had already received an account in which her son was available.

Mariana takes the two sheets. “The vendor will know which shop is above this.”

“He has the first contract,” Peter says.

“Then we ask him to bring it.”

I put the study reports at the back of the envelope. On top I leave Leda's signed page, with the wet mark beneath the line and the objection she made them add. We have an address to go to, and this time I know whose question I am carrying with mine.
